Dear Police Commissioner. Service Review: Next Time, Could I Call You?

Open letter. First published on LinkedIn.

Police Commissioner,

A copy of WA’s updated family violence legislation sits pinned to my homepage like a gold star on the State’s report card. I read the press releases. I read the policy documents. They are inspiring. Progressive.

What they are not, apparently, is instructive.

And you cannot ride on progress’s forward momentum when there is none.

There is none.

Escalating violence. My neighbour, unrecognisable. Three calls to 000. Holes in walls. Broken items. Broken bones. Named and willing witnesses.

And the words out of your officer’s mouth:

“She doesn’t want anything to do with the bloke. But she doesn’t want to press charges. I don’t get it.”

I do not need to tell you that victims do not "press charges" in this state.

That distinction matters most in family violence, don’t you agree? Particularly when fear may force a victim to blame the door for injuries she cannot hide.

Police investigate. Police decide whether to lay charges. The procedure is straightforward. Black and white. Right?

An attending officer should not need a frightened witness to explain why an injured woman may want nothing more to do with the man who assaulted her, yet still feel unable to cooperate with police.

That is not a contradiction.

That is family violence.

That night was a failure of training. Or was that night the training?

From where I stood, with the decision to leave being made, then unmade only because I refused to let it happen quietly, it did not look like an exception.

It looked like policy.

WA is changing the way it talks about family violence. You now speak about coercive control, patterns of abuse, cumulative harm and risk.

Commissioner, your officers read the scene about as well as they read the memo: fear can look like silence, denial or withdrawal.

A damaged home. A victim too scared to speak. A perpetrator no longer present. An identified and willing witness.

All of that about to be concluded with your officers, on their way out, calling into the home of the person who called them over.

Note the “about to.”

They did stay a little longer, but only after I met their ludicrous decision with an equally ludicrous question:

“Should I simply call 000 again once you go, so you come back?”

That question should not have been necessary.

I had called 000. I waited. I intervened while I waited. When the violence escalated, I intervened again, the perpetrator turned on me. I called twice more and gave precise, real-time accounts of the brutality being unleashed on my neighbour.

Yet no one asked me for a formal statement. No one left a card or a name or a number. No questions were asked about what “he turned on me” actually meant.

Between my words and her injuries, we still did not reach whatever threshold your officers needed before family violence protocols kicked in.

Perhaps it would have been had I put on headphones, gone to sleep and called in a dead body on my driveway the next morning?

The new legislation is inspiring. It is progressive.

It is not instructive.

Commissioner, six years ago, when I the victim of life changing domestic violence, I asked two of your officers:

“How can I expect my neighbour to call police when police can’t even be bothered turning up?”

I am the neighbour now.

I called.

They showed up.

And I am asking why they bothered.
