Dear Neighbour: Tell me when to call

Creative nonfiction. First published on LinkedIn. The people involved are not named.

Dear neighbour,

I’m worried.

I’m worried that lately the “F--- yoooooooooouuuuuuu” he screams at you lasts much longer than it used to.

I’m worried that, unlike your other neighbours, I know what every extra syllable means.

It is no longer a phrase. It is every supposed wrongdoing he has stored against you. Every grievance he has lodged. Every justification he is building to permit what happens next.

The longer the word, the shorter his fuse, the greater the risk.

I know too well what happens when that fuse is lit. What comes next frightens us more than we will ever confess.

Rage can be loud, but the moment it turns deadly is often quiet.

Like the night I did not recognise you.

We have been neighbours long enough for me to know your face. Yet that night, in the dark, I thought I was running towards a stranger, a cousin, a sister, an auntie or a friend.

It was not until you spoke that I realised it was you.

Now I sit on the other side of a brick wall trying to calculate the exact moment concern becomes an emergency.

Call too early and police arrive, hear calm voices, decide there is nothing to see and leave.

He always punished me after they left.

Call too late and the ambulance will have no reason to hurry.

Last time, I had to call more than once. When they finally arrived, it had taken longer than the 12-minute priority-response target. I then spent longer than that reasoning with them to make them stay.

Then I had to calmly explain to an officer who genuinely did not seem to know why you “didn’t want to press charges.”

I am scared that next time I will have to explain why you are leaving in a coroner’s van rather than an ambulance.

I have enough memories of moments when somebody should have acted sooner. I do not need another life measured afterwards through warning signs everyone suddenly claims were obvious.

I do not want you to stay here as a ghost.

I do not know whether silence respects your choices or abandons you to his.

I know the warnings about stepping in. I know the danger of interfering.

I know why.

A second injured or dead woman does not rescue the first.

I know there are risks in calling. I know there are reasons you may fear the people sent to help you. I cannot pretend those reasons are small.

Yet death does not become less final through details.

If you die, you are still dead.

You become another woman discussed in the past tense. Another statistic. Another death met with promises to improve a response that needed its hand held while it lost you.

I will intervene.

So I need a word.

Not an explanation.

Not permission.

Not a safety plan.

A word.

“Pyjamas.”

“Banana.”

“Fire.”

Anything.

Any word that tells me you have reached the point where your fear outweighs your fight.

A word that means: call now.

You should not have to carry the extra task of creating an emergency protocol for the neighbour listening through the wall.

Yet here I am asking you to do exactly that.

Because I have to.

When help arrives carrying the judgement of an officer who was taught about domestic violence but did not learn it, arriving early may permit the violence rather than stop it.

Without a word, I am left listening for the difference between another beating and your last breath.

I do not know what you want me to do.

We share no friendship, no family connection and very few words.

We share a brick wall somewhere in between two places that we hoped would be safe.

Yet I know his voice.

I know it is changing.

I know the word is getting longer.

I know your time may be getting shorter.

Dear neighbour,

Please tell me when to call.

Before the silence settles and your ghost starts paying rent.

In an emergency, call 000.

Not an emergency but worried about your neighbour? Call 1800RESPECT.
