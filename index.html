Submission · Lead author, for DVAA

Submission to the NSW statutory review of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024

Prepared by: Charlee Stockden, Advocacy & Policy Director, Domestic Violence Advocacy Australia

Lodged with: NSW Department of Communities and Justice, 2026

My role: I wrote this submission, except Questions 5 and 6, which the DVAA Chair began and I completed.

Acknowledgment of Country

Domestic Violence Advocacy Australia acknowledges the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples as the First Nations peoples of this country and the Traditional Custodians of the lands, waters and seas on which we live and work. We pay our deep respects to Elders past and present, and honour their enduring cultural, spiritual and custodial connections to Country.

We recognise that the violence experienced by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples today cannot be separated from the ongoing impacts of colonisation, dispossession, forced child removal, systemic racism and intergenerational trauma. First Nations peoples are disproportionately harmed by family, domestic and sexual violence. They are overrepresented in systems that respond after harm has occurred, rather than being resourced to prevent violence and support healing led by community.

Domestic Violence Advocacy Australia acknowledges that addressing family violence requires truth-telling, self-determination, and genuine partnership with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. We are committed to advocating for responses that are culturally safe, community-led, trauma-informed and grounded in justice, recognising that the safety and wellbeing of First Nations peoples is inseparable from broader efforts to end domestic and family violence in Australia.

Who we are

This submission is prepared by Domestic Violence Advocacy Australia Ltd (DVAA).

DVAA aims to amplify the voices of those with lived experience of domestic, family and sexual violence, driving transformative change in policies, legislation and societal attitudes across Australia by uniting survivors, fostering an inclusive community of support, resilience, collective action and healing.

We lead change by advocating for the rights and needs of domestic, family and sexual violence victims by building a national movement that empowers sharing lived experiences to influence local, state, and national policies. Ensuring that victims' experiences shape safer, more compassionate communities, with a focus on prevention and deterrents for perpetrators through survivor-led campaigns.

The stories of victim-survivors, told and untold, are at the heart of our work. These include those of children and young people, whom DVAA recognises as victim-survivors in their own right. We remember the lives that have been lost, and stand with those who continue to navigate the impacts of violence. We value the knowledge and contribution of victim survivors to advocacy and systemic reform, and are deeply grateful to those who helped inform this submission.

Introduction

Domestic Violence Advocacy Australia welcomes the opportunity to contribute to the statutory review of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024. We commend the NSW Government for its commitment to evaluating these reforms and for seeking stakeholders' views on how they operate in practice.

As an organisation informed by the experiences and perspectives of victim-survivors, DVAA recognises the importance of legislation that not only establishes legal protections but translates those protections into meaningful improvements in safety, accountability, and access to justice. The experiences of victim-survivors provide an important perspective on how legislative reforms are understood, accessed, and implemented, and whether further action may be needed to achieve their intended outcomes.

Whilst the policy objectives of these reforms remain valid, there is a gap between the protection the law provides and the protection it delivers. Victim-survivors describe breaches that are not acted on, and a reluctance to report when earlier reports went unanswered. They also describe technology-facilitated monitoring that is not recognised as stalking, and receiving an order without the information or support needed to feel safe.

The available data reflects these concerns. Around one in three charges for breaching an order to cause harm or fear is withdrawn. As at 29 September 2026, only one Serious Domestic Abuse Prevention Order had been made.

We believe these reforms provide a strong foundation. With clear guidance, consistent enforcement and dedicated resourcing, they can deliver the protection Parliament intended.

We ask the Government to give particular weight to the voices of those who live with the consequences of these reforms day to day. Their experiences should be considered alongside available data to help identify where reforms are working, where gaps remain, and what practical changes could strengthen their effectiveness.

DVAA thanks the Department of Communities and Justice for considering this submission and would welcome the opportunity to discuss it further.

Aggravated ADVO breach offences

Consultation Paper, Chapter 2

Question 1: Do the policy objectives of sections 14(1A) to (1E) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain valid?

Response to Question 1:

Yes, the policy objectives remain valid. When the offences were introduced, the Attorney General said that they would help the law better “differentiate between breaches and recognise that some are more serious.” We support this. Breaching an order to cause harm or fear, or breaching it again and again, puts victim-survivors at greater risk, and the law should say so.

It is our view that a tiered approach is just, but its implementation falls short. People with lived experience continue to find that reports of breaches are not taken seriously by police. Victim-survivors also still feel that reporting a breach increases their risk.

The consultation paper shows that around one in three charges under section 14(1A) and around one in four under section 14(1C) were withdrawn by the prosecution, often as part of a negotiated resolution. We ask that the review look at why these charges were withdrawn. Where a victim-survivor no longer wants to proceed, it should ask whether fear for their safety played a part and whether the law could have protected them.

From arrest to finalisation, the process is, on average, too long to keep victim-survivors safe unless bail is refused for that whole time. The consultation paper shows that aggravated breach matters take close to 6 months on average to finalise, and closer to 10 months when they go to a defended hearing. That is a long time for a victim-survivor to wait, often while still living in fear.

The consultation paper shows that Aboriginal people make up around 29% of defendants across all breach offences, and that this proportion has not increased under the aggravated offences. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people remain overrepresented in the justice system and also disproportionately harmed by family violence. We ask that the review continue to monitor this and that the responses to breaches include culturally safe, community-led programs that address the behaviour alongside protections for Aboriginal victim-survivors.

Recognising that some breaches carry greater risk is the right objective. For it to be met in practice, victim-survivors need to be safe while their matter moves through the system and confident that reporting a breach will lead to action.

Question 2: Do the terms of sections 14(1A) to (1E) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain appropriate to secure their objectives?

Response to Question 2:

Partly. We support the aggravated breach offences and want them to stay, but lived experience tells us that some of the terms and their use leave victim-survivors without the protection the offences were meant to provide.

The 28-day window is too short. Section 14(1C) applies only when a person has breached the order at least twice in the 28 days before. Abusers often spread breaches out over weeks or months. Each breach looks small on its own, so it flies under the radar, but together they wear the victim-survivor down and keep them in a state of fear. A pattern of breaches is dangerous whenever it happens. Every breach of the same order should count, for as long as the order is in place.

Every breach must be taken seriously. Any breach shows that the person is willing to ignore a court order and should be addressed accordingly.

Advocates say people already hold back from reporting a breach when it seems small, when earlier reports were not acted on, or when they do not think the police will act. The serious breach offences should be used alongside a firm response to every breach, not instead of one.

We are concerned that the serious offences are not being used as often as they could be. The consultation paper shows that fewer than 1 in 40 finalised breach charges were laid under each of the aggravated offences, and many of those are withdrawn, often as part of a negotiated outcome. Victim-survivors report that police often treat repeated breaches as separate small incidents instead of a danger sign. The law can only work if police and prosecutors recognise the patterns and lay charges.

We recommend

  1. Keep the aggravated breach offences.

  2. Remove the 28-day window in section 14(1C) so that the repeated breaches at any time during the life of the order can support an aggravated charge.

  3. Record and investigate every reported breach so a pattern of small breaches is on record and can support an aggravated charge.

  4. Give police clear written guidance on when to charge an aggravated breach, especially when there is a pattern of repeated breaches.

  5. Train police to see repeated breaches as a danger sign, not as small separate incidents.

  6. Publish yearly data on how many breaches are reported, charged, withdrawn, proven, and the sentences given.

Question 3: Do you have concerns about the operation of sections 14(1D) and (1E) when an aggravated offence is tried on indictment?

Response to Question 3:

Yes, we are concerned about how sections 14(1D) and 14(1E) work when a serious breach case is heard in a higher court.

We support the aggravated breach offences. They recognise that breaching an order to cause harm or fear, or breaching it repeatedly, puts victim-survivors at greater risk. We also support the fallback in sections 14(1D) and 14(1E). If the court is not satisfied the serious offence is proven, it can still find the person guilty of the ordinary breach. That fallback means a person who clearly broke the order is still held to account.

The problem is that the fallback does not work the same way in every court. The ordinary breach offences can only be heard in the local court. When a serious breach case is heard in the district court, the court may not be able to fallback onto the ordinary breach. If the serious charges are not proven, the person could walk free completely even when everyone agrees they broke the order.

This matters to victim-survivors because reporting a breach takes a great deal of effort. Lived experience shows that many people already hold back from reporting because they fear what the person will do, because early reports were not acted on, or because they do not think police will act. When a victim-survivor finds the courage to report, gives evidence, and goes through a court process, the outcome should not be lost because of which court heard the case.

Victim-survivors say that ADVOs are just pieces of paper to perpetrators and they only become truly protective when police record and prosecute breaches. A breach that is reported, charged, and proven but still ends in an acquittal is exactly the kind of outcome that evidences the idea that an order cannot protect a victim. The serious breach offences were introduced to give victim-survivors stronger protection. They should not create a situation in which charging the more serious offence makes a person less likely to be held to account than if only the ordinary breach had been charged.

Question 4: If yes to Question 3, are existing procedures, including the use of back up or related charges, sufficient to address any concern?

Response to Question 4:

No. We do not think backup charges are enough on their own.

We welcome the aggravated breach offences. They show that Parliament understands some breaches are more dangerous than others. But these offences only protect victim-survivors if they cannot leave a gap where the person walks free.

At the moment, the fallback to the ordinary breach offence depends on prosecutors laying a backup charge at the start of every case that goes to a higher court. If that step is missed, even once, a person who clearly broke the order could be acquitted completely. A victim-survivor's safety should not depend on a step that relies on someone remembering it every time. Prosecutors work under heavy pressure and high caseloads, and mistakes happen. The law should be built so that a mistake does not cost a victim-survivor their protection.

The consultation paper itself notes that relying on backup charges does not fully give effect to Parliament's intention that the fallback be available "as of right" in every case. We agree. If Parliament intended the fallback to always be there, the law should make sure it always is.

This matters because of what happens when a breach has no consequence. Lived experience tells us that when breaches go unpunished, the person learns they can keep going. Some former partners breach orders repeatedly without serious consequences, and then escalate because they know they can. Advocates say that cheap fines for repeated breaches are not a deterrent. An acquittal on a technicality sends the same message, to the person who breached and to the victim-survivor, who may decide it is not worth reporting next time.

We recommend:

  1. Change the law so the court can always find a person guilty of the ordinary breach offence when a serious breach charge is not proven, including when the case is heard in a higher court.

  2. Until the law is changed, require prosecutors to lay a backup charge of the ordinary breach in every serious breach case that goes to a higher court. This should be written into prosecution guidelines as a standard step, not left to individual judgement.

  3. Review other offences that may have the same problem, as the consultation paper suggests, so that the same gap does not leave victim-survivors unprotected under other laws.

Serious Domestic Abuse Prevention Orders

Consultation Paper, Chapter 3

Question 5: Do the policy objectives of Part 10A of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain valid?

Response to Question 5:

The policy objectives of Part 10A remain valid, despite limited use of the SDAPO to date.

Unlike Clare’s Law, which focuses on a partner's right to know, the SDAPO places the burden of stopping the criminal behaviour on the person engaging in that harmful behaviour, focusing on the right to protection. However, this protection does not exceed 5 years, which may limit its value for victim-survivors and the Police Commissioner, as there is no evidence that rehabilitation takes only 5 years (noting that another court application can be brought against the same person).

Possibly, the reason for low applications is that it requires a proactive approach to stopping domestic violence before it starts, which is an unfamiliar approach for the justice system.

Should the SDAPO be dropped from legislation, an additional offence or means of holding people using domestic violence accountable to stop their behaviour before it happens again should be undertaken, such as Clare’s Law. It should be a worse crime to repeat domestic violence offences; therefore, having an SDAPO could be a mechanism for holding serial domestic violence offenders to account.

The intention of the SDAPO is commendable, but the implementation does not ensure effective use of the justice system to minimise recidivism. For it to protect future partners, implementation needs to be strengthened with clear operational guidance so police and prosecutors use these orders as Parliament intended. If the scheme is not retained, it should be replaced with another mechanism that holds serious repeat offenders to account before further partners are harmed.

We recommend:

  1. Keep Part 10A.

  2. Give police and the DPP clear operational guidance on when and how to apply for an SDAPO, so the scheme is used for people it was designed for.

  3. Make it simpler for police to apply for the further SDAPO when an order is close to expiring, and the risk continues.

  4. Publish yearly data on SDAPO applications, orders made, conditions, and outcomes.

Question 6: Do the terms of Part 10A of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain appropriate?

Response to Question 6:

Yes. The core terms of Part 10A remain appropriate, particularly that an order applies to people aged 18 years and over who have engaged in serious domestic abuse activity, and that the court must be satisfied the order will protect former, current, and potential partners.

Requiring the court to consider the safety of potential future partners alongside current and former partners is a vital safeguard against serial abuse. However, the threshold criteria will only protect future partners if police and prosecutors have clear operational guidance on the evidence required to meet those terms, so that eligible high-risk offenders are actually brought before the court.

Question 7: Do you have views about: the scope of conditions that may be imposed under a SDAPO, the timing requirement for service of a SDAPO application on the defendant, and/or the interaction between service requirements of a SDAPO made by the court and the SDAPO contravention offence?

Response to Question 7a:

We support the court being able to set broad conditions tailored to each person's behaviour. But the scope of conditions is not the main concern for victim-survivors. The main concern is whether the conditions will be used, checked, and enforced.

The consultation paper states that, as at 29 September 2026, the police made only four SDAPO applications, the Director of Public Prosecutions made none, and only one order has been made. A scheme designed to protect future partners from people with a serious history of domestic violence cannot do that if it's not being used.

Conditions such as reporting to police, telling police about a new relationship or change of address, staying off dating apps, or not using tracking devices are only meaningful if someone is checking on them. The law does not say who is responsible for monitoring compliance or how it will be resourced. Lived experience tells us that police already struggle to respond to breaches of ordinary orders and often do not understand the technology involved. Without a clear plan for monitoring and enforcement, broad conditions risk becoming another piece of paper.

We recommend:

  1. Keep the broad scope of conditions so orders can be tailored to each person's behaviour.

  2. Make clear who is responsible for monitoring compliance with SDAPO conditions and fund them to do it.

  3. Give police specialist support, including technology expertise, to monitor conditions involving devices, apps, and online accounts.

  4. Give police and the DPP clear guidance on when to apply for SDAPO, so the scheme is used for the people it was designed for.

  5. Publish data each year on applications, orders made, conditions imposed, breaches, and outcomes, so the community can see whether the scheme is working.

Response to Question 7b:

We do not object to a longer notice period if it is needed for procedural fairness. The court can already set a different timeframe, and we would support its use where a respondent genuinely needs more time to seek legal advice.

Our concern is the safety of victim-survivors while the application is pending. A longer notice period gives the person using violence more time before an order can be made, and more time to prepare to contest it. Any extension, such as to 21 days, should only apply where an AVO is already in place to protect the victim-survivor in the meantime.

Response to Question 7c:

We support amending section 87D(1)(a) so that a SDAPO takes effect when it is made if the respondent is present in court. Where the respondent is not present, the order should be able to be served by email or text message not only in person.

A person who is in court when the order is made knows what it requires of them. Their obligations should begin at that moment. Protection should not depend on whether a copy is personally handed to them afterwards. At present, a person can leave court knowing the terms of the order and still not be bound by it.

A person who avoids the court should not be able to avoid the order. Some of the most dangerous respondents may choose not to attend, and then avoid personal service, so the order never takes effect. Allowing service by email or text message would close this gap.

We also recommend that the SDAPO application warn the respondent that failing to appear in court will not allow them to avoid complying with any order made in their absence.

This gap may also discourage applications. Preparing an SDAPO application takes considerable police time and resources. That effort is wasted if an order is made but cannot be enforced because it has not yet been served.

Amended definition of ‘stalking’

Consultation Paper, Chapter 4

Question 8: Do the policy objectives of section 8(1)(b1) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain valid?

Response to Question 8:

Yes, the objective is still valid, and our community's experience shows how much it is needed.

We welcome this change. Before it, the law defined stalking as following someone, watching or hanging around their home or workplace or by contacting or approaching them, including by phone message, email or online. Technology was only covered when it was used to contact the person. But many victim-survivors are stalked without the person ever contacting or coming near them. A hidden tracker on a car, spyware on a phone, or secret access to someone's email accounts can let a person watch every move a victim-survivor makes without sending a single message. The new definition recognises that tracking or monitoring someone is stalking, whether it is done with technology or another way and whether or not the person makes contact. Being tracked or monitored by a family member, partner, or former partner is common. Victim-survivors describe tracking devices, spyware, monitoring software, fake online accounts, messaging apps, shared passwords, and access to their accounts.

We hear stories of former partners connecting their devices to a victim-survivor's emails, reading everything she/they send, always knowing where they are, and still holding all their passwords even while an order is in place. This makes it unsafe for victim-survivors to reach out to support services at all.

Lived experience shows us why the old law is not enough. Reporting, tracking, or monitoring to the police is often met with the response that it was not a crime unless there was direct contact or a threat. When the law only recognises contact, a person can watch, track, and control the victim-survivor's whole life without breaking an ADVO. This conduct is not harmless. Australian research in Victoria found that nearly two-thirds of the homicides of former partners it examined were preceded by stalking. (Sheed, Brandt & McEwan, 2026). The objective to capture tracking and monitoring without needing direct contact remains valid and important.

Question 9: Do the terms of section 8(1)(b1) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain appropriate?

Response to Question 9:

We support maintaining the broad wording. However, lived experience tells us that the law is only as strong as the people applying it, and that police and courts need more help in recognising such conduct.

The words, “whether by using technology or in another way,” are a strength. Technology changes quickly, and broad wording means new forms of tracking are covered. Advocates agree that a fixed list of examples could leave out harmful conduct or become outdated quickly as technology changes. We agree that any examples must not limit that definition.

However, police often do not understand the technology and do not take it seriously. We hear stories of police recording what happened but not taking a statement, leaving insufficient evidence to renew an order even when the risk is clear.

Victim-survivors strongly support the law giving clear examples of this conduct. Advocates say examples would make clear the conduct is wrong, help police and courts recognise it, help victim-survivors identify and report it and reduce the chance it is dismissed as harmless.

The consultation paper notes there has been no case law on this provision yet. Without examples or guidance, it is left to individual police officers to decide what counts. That is where the system lets victims and survivors down.

We recommend:

  1. Keep the broad definition, including the words "or in another way," so new technology and new behaviour are always covered.

  2. Add examples to the law, making clear they are examples only and do not limit the definition. These should include GPS trackers and Air Tags, spyware and monitoring apps, checking messages or browser history, accessing or monitoring online accounts or accessing passwords, and using another person or system to monitor someone.

  3. Train police to recognise and investigate technology-facilitated stalking, including how to collect evidence from devices and accounts.

  4. Give courts guidance on how to understand these cases.

  5. Collect and publish data on technology-facilitated stalking charges and what happens to them so the review can see whether the change is working.

Procedural reforms for provisional AVOs

Consultation Paper, Chapter 5

Question 10: Do the policy objectives of sections 31 and 32 of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain valid?

Response to Question 10:

Yes, both objectives remain valid. Section 31 allows police to serve a provisional order by email or phone, provided the person agrees and an officer has explained the order to them in person. The aim is to make service faster and avoid holding people at the roadside or bringing them back to a police station just to hand over paper. We support this.

An order cannot protect anyone until it's served, and a person cannot be charged with breaching an order they have not been served with. Every delay in service time is time a victim-survivor is left without protection. Section 32 ensures a victim-survivor remains protected by the provisional order until the new court order is served. We strongly support this.

Lived experience tells us that the time around court dates is often when a victim-survivor feels most at risk. A gap in protection, even a short one, can be dangerous.

An order only works when the person it is made against understands what they must not do. We hear stories of people who seem to only partly understand their order or who use that uncertainty as an excuse. This is why the requirement for an officer to explain the order in person is so important.

Faster service is welcome, but it must never become a tick-box exercise where the explanation is rushed or skipped. The objectives of sections 31 and 32 remain valid because they keep the focus where it belongs: making sure victim-survivors are protected as quickly as possible and that protection does not lapse.

Question 11: Do the terms of sections 31 and 32 of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain appropriate?

Response to Question 11:

Yes, the terms are broadly appropriate. We commend the safeguards already in Section 31. Electronic service can only happen if the person agrees and only after a police officer has explained in person what the order means, what happens if it is breached, and what rights each person has. These are the right protections, and they should stay. We also support Section 32 as written. A victim-survivor should never lose protection because a new order has not yet been served. However, lived experience tells us that having an order is not the same as feeling safe or supported. Many victim-survivors report that they did not have the information and support they needed once an order was made, and that the order often did not make them feel safer. The period when an order is applied for and served is when victim-survivors have the most contact with police. Police should use this time to connect them with support services, rather than treating it only as an administrative task.

We recommend:

  1. Keep the existing safeguards in Section 31, including consent and the in-person explanation.

  2. Send a plain-language summary with every electronically served order, explaining what the order means, what constitutes a breach, and what to do if it is breached, so that both parties have it in writing.

  3. Include support information with every order served on a protected person, such as local domestic violence services, court support, and legal help.

  4. Make sure protected people know that they can say no to electronic service and ask to be served in person instead.

  5. Record when and how the in-person explanation is given so that it can be checked and cannot be skipped.

  6. Ask protected people about their experience of electronic service as part of this review so the review looks at how it feels for victim-survivors and not only how it is used.

Any other comments

In addition to the reforms under review, DVAA asks the NSW Government to consider the following measures to strengthen long-term safety and accountability.

Post-custody protection for serious offenders

DVAA commends the NSW Government for enabling courts to make indefinite ADVOs and to take a defendant's imprisonment into account when deciding how long an order should last. These provisions recognise that risk does not end when a sentence does. However, an indefinite order can only be made when one is requested. Survivor-led recovery cannot begin while a victim-survivor faces the prospect of an order expiring just as the person who harmed them returns to the community.

We recommend:

  1. Police routinely request an indefinite order, or an order that continues beyond release, when a person is imprisoned for serious domestic violence offences.

  2. The Government publish yearly data on how many indefinite ADVOs are requested and made, and how many orders continue beyond a defendant's release from custody.

Reducing the burden of renewal on victim-survivors

Victim-survivors whose orders are not indefinite must return to court each time they need an extension. Each application requires them to present their evidence again and, in many cases, to face the person who harmed them. This places responsibility for ongoing safety on the very person the order exists to protect. Lived experience tells us that some victim-survivors do not seek an extension because the process is too demanding, even when the risk has not changed.

DVAA asks why the onus is on the victim-survivor to secure their own safety. Where risk is ongoing, responsibility for maintaining protection should rest with the system.

We recommend:

  1. Police apply for extensions on behalf of victim-survivors where risk remains, so the victim-survivor is not required to start each application.

  2. A simplified extension process that relies on the existing record, so victim-survivors are not required to prove their case again from the beginning.

Critique of an independent medical examination

Letter critiquing an insurer-arranged Independent Medical Examination in a workers’ compensation matter, written to support the claimant’s lawyer. De-identified: names, dates, the insurer and the hospital have been removed. Not medical or legal advice.

Letter: Critique of Independent Medical Examination

Re: [Claimant] – Independent Medical Examination Report of [Examining doctor] dated [date]

Dear [Solicitor]

I write regarding the Independent Medical Examination report of [Examining doctor] dated [date] prepared at the request of [the insurer].

Having reviewed the report carefully, there are serious concerns regarding the factual accuracy, reasoning and medical conclusions reached. In my view, the report materially understates objective evidence consistent with carbon monoxide exposure and its aftermath, and then relies on that understatement to substitute an unsupported diagnosis of Somatic Symptom Disorder.

For the reasons outlined below, the report appears vulnerable to challenge on both evidentiary and clinical grounds.

1. The report incorrectly asserts an absence of objective evidence of carbon monoxide exposure

The examining doctor states that there is:

  • “no objective evidence confirming significant carbon monoxide exposure”
  • “no documented measurement of carboxyhaemoglobin levels”
  • “no contemporaneous medical assessment at the time of the alleged exposure”.

However, the report itself records several objective matters which contradict that statement.

These include:

  • the treating hospital identifying the principal diagnosis as carbon monoxide inhalation/poisoning
  • a venous blood gas measurement recording carbon monoxide level of 6.5
  • referral for and completion of six sessions of hyperbaric oxygen therapy
  • MMSE improvement from 24/30 to 30/30 following hyperbaric treatment
  • improvement in Romberg testing following treatment

These are objective clinical findings. Regardless of how one interprets them, it is plainly inaccurate to state there was “no documented measurement” and no objective evidence.

2. The report fails to properly consider the delayed diagnosis and treatment

The chronology recorded within the report itself is significant:

  • prolonged exhaust exposure under a running vehicle (2–3 hours)
  • immediate neurological symptoms on emerging
  • presentation to hospital the same day
  • a five-hour wait without medical assessment
  • the patient leaving prior to assessment
  • later hospital review and treatment

Carbon monoxide levels decline over time, particularly once oxygen is administered. Consequently, delayed blood gas testing may underestimate the severity of the original exposure.

More importantly, delayed recognition and treatment may influence neurological outcome. The report does not adequately engage with this issue.

3. A normal MRI does not exclude carbon monoxide related neurological injury

The report places considerable reliance on the absence of MRI abnormalities.

That reliance is overstated.

While MRI can demonstrate characteristic injury patterns in some carbon monoxide exposures, the absence of such findings does not exclude neurological sequelae. Cognitive, behavioural and functional impairments may persist even where routine neuroimaging appears normal.

The absence of globus pallidus lesions or white matter abnormalities cannot reasonably be used as definitive proof that no injury occurred.

4. A normal neurological examination does not exclude functional impairment

The report relies heavily on the fact that the neurological examination on [date] was described as normal.

However, a largely normal bedside neurological examination months after toxic exposure does not exclude:

  • cognitive impairment
  • executive dysfunction
  • slowed processing speed
  • fatigue syndromes
  • neurobehavioural changes
  • subtle gait or balance impairment.

Such impairments frequently require formal neuropsychological assessment rather than routine clinical examination.

The report does not meaningfully address this distinction.

5. Objective findings recorded in the medical records are not adequately reconciled with the conclusion

The report acknowledges several findings that appear inconsistent with the final conclusion but does not adequately explain them.

These include:

  • MMSE initially 24/30
  • improvement to 30/30 following hyperbaric treatment
  • abnormal Romberg test
  • documented gait instability
  • persistent fatigue
  • observed stuttering
  • slow gait with difficulty on heel-to-toe walking
  • inability to safely complete workplace trial duties due to fatigue.

Taken together, these findings indicate that there were objective abnormalities following the incident.

The report acknowledges these findings but effectively dismisses them without proper explanation.

6. The proposed alternative diagnosis does not explain the clinical presentation

The examining doctor suggests that the claimant “probably had an episode of acute peripheral vestibulopathy”.

This hypothesis fails to explain numerous features recorded in the report, including:

  • profound drowsiness and inability to keep eyes open
  • cognitive slowing
  • memory impairment
  • speech disturbance
  • documented MMSE change
  • improvement following hyperbaric treatment
  • exertional suffocation sensation
  • new intolerance to exhaust fumes
  • persistent fatigue and functional impairment.

Peripheral vestibular disorders may explain vertigo. They do not adequately explain the broader constellation of symptoms described.

7. The diagnosis of Somatic Symptom Disorder is not properly supported

The report concludes that the claimant’s current symptoms are best explained by Somatic Symptom Disorder.

However, the report does not set out the diagnostic criteria used or demonstrate that those criteria are satisfied.

The diagnosis appears to be based primarily on the absence of MRI findings and a largely normal neurological examination. That is not sufficient to justify such a diagnosis.

Importantly, the report does not demonstrate that the claimant exhibits the type of disproportionate symptom preoccupation required to support this diagnosis.

Frustration, distress and concern about persistent symptoms following a documented workplace exposure do not, by themselves, constitute a somatic symptom disorder.

8. The report understates the recognised neurological consequences of carbon monoxide exposure

The report suggests that the persistence of symptoms across multiple domains is not typical of carbon monoxide poisoning.

This assertion is questionable.

Carbon monoxide exposure is well known to produce:

  • cognitive impairment
  • gait disturbance
  • fatigue
  • mood and behavioural change
  • executive dysfunction
  • delayed neurological sequelae.

The symptoms recorded in the report itself are not inconsistent with that spectrum.

9. The reasoning regarding work capacity is internally inconsistent

The report states that the claimant has recovered completely from any neurological injury and can return to full duties without restriction.

However, the report simultaneously states that his symptoms (attributed to Somatic Symptom Disorder) currently prevent successful return to work.

These statements are difficult to reconcile. If the claimant remains functionally impaired to the point of being unable to work successfully, that impairment requires proper clinical explanation rather than simple reclassification.

10. The occupational context has not been adequately addressed

The history recorded by the examining doctor describes:

  • prolonged exhaust exposure under a running vehicle
  • immediate onset of neurological symptoms
  • subsequent hospital diagnosis of carbon monoxide poisoning
  • hyperbaric treatment
  • objective improvement following treatment.

This sequence strongly supports the occurrence of a toxic exposure event.

The report does not adequately explain why this chain of events should be regarded as unrelated to the ongoing symptoms.

Conclusion

The difficulty with this report is not merely that it reaches an unfavourable opinion.

Rather, the report appears to:

  • minimise or mischaracterise objective evidence
  • over-rely on normal MRI findings
  • over-rely on a largely normal office neurological examination
  • fail to adequately consider delayed diagnosis and treatment
  • substitute Somatic Symptom Disorder without establishing diagnostic criteria.

In those circumstances, the report appears open to challenge.

It may be appropriate to obtain a further opinion from a specialist with experience in:

  • carbon monoxide neurotoxicity
  • acquired brain injury
  • neuropsychology
  • neuropsychiatry
  • occupational neurology.

In particular, such an expert could address whether residual cognitive and functional impairments may persist despite normal MRI findings and a largely normal bedside neurological examination.

Given the issues outlined above, the reasoning and conclusions contained in the IME report appear contestable.

Yours sincerely

Charlee

For [Claimant]

Urgent protective intervention guide: suspected elder financial abuse

Anonymised and edited portfolio version. All names, addresses, dates of birth, contact details and case-specific identifiers have been removed or generalised. Not legal advice.

Purpose: This sample demonstrates practical advocacy, procedural literacy, evidence organisation, plain-English drafting, and urgent risk mapping in a high-stakes administrative/legal context.

1. Portfolio snapshot

Element Summary
Document type Plain-English intervention guide and supporting document suite

Audience Family advocates, support workers, tribunal-facing support roles, policy/complaints teams, community legal or elder abuse services

Boundary The document is an advocacy and systems-navigation sample. It does not present legal advice and should be reviewed by a qualified lawyer before real-world use.

2. Case context

An older person was believed to be subject to financial risk after a family member acting under an Enduring Power of Attorney was said to be progressing the sale of the older person’s home. The family also raised concerns about restricted contact, lack of financial transparency, possible misuse of care-related funds, and uncertainty about the older person’s current wishes and decision-making capacity.

The immediate concern was not to prove every allegation at the first step. The immediate concern was to preserve the position long enough for proper review by the appropriate oversight bodies.

Central risk: the property could be sold, and sale proceeds could become difficult to trace or recover, before capacity, authority and financial management concerns were independently reviewed.

3. Strategic objective

The practical objective was to slow, pause or prevent irreversible action while creating a clear paper trail and escalating the matter to appropriate protective channels.

  • Preserve evidence: collect documents, messages, records, witness accounts and sale information.
  • Notify the correct bodies: raise the matter with the relevant public advocate/guardianship and tribunal pathways.
  • Disrupt the sale process lawfully: place the selling side on written notice that authority and instructions were disputed.
  • Seek urgent protective intervention: request review, production of records, and interim protection of the asset pending proper assessment.

4. Four-track action model

Track Action
Track 1: Preserve evidence Build a central evidence folder. Collect EPA documents, property listing records, care placement information, financial concerns, contact restriction evidence, witness statements and correspondence logs.
Track 2: Notify oversight bodies Prepare a concise complaint/referral pack for the relevant public advocate/elder abuse pathway and tribunal registry. Keep the first filing focused on urgency, capacity, authority and asset protection.
Track 3: Place sale parties on notice Send a formal written notice to the real estate agent and, if known, settlement/conveyancing parties. Request that no contracts, settlement steps or sale activity proceed while authority is disputed and urgent review is being sought.
Track 4: Seek protective orders Ask the tribunal or relevant decision-maker to consider interim protection, review of the attorney’s authority, production of financial records, and appointment of an alternative decision-maker if required.

5. Urgent execution plan

  1. Create a master folder. Separate material into evidence categories such as EPA documents, property sale, care placement, financial records, witness statements and correspondence.
  2. Prepare a one-page chronology. Record key dates: authority granted, contact restrictions, care changes, property listing, alleged payment demand and any urgent sale activity.
  3. Gather core documents. Prioritise documents that prove authority, sale activity, care placement, finances, payments, rent, utility records and communications.
  4. Prepare witness statements. Each person should state what they personally saw, heard or received. Avoid assumptions.
  5. Finalise the public advocate complaint. Attach the chronology and key supporting records.
  6. Prepare tribunal material. Include a cover letter, supporting statement, chronology and clearly labelled annexures.
  7. Send formal notice to the selling agent. Request preservation of documents and pause of sale steps pending review.
  8. Send notice to settlement or conveyancing parties if known. Keep proof of sending.
  9. Keep everything in writing. Follow up phone calls with same-day written summaries.
  10. Track responses. Use a response log showing recipient, date sent, document sent, response received and next action needed.
  11. Escalate if the sale moves quickly. If there is a live offer, contract preparation or settlement movement, immediately update the oversight bodies.
  12. Keep the message consistent. The focus is protection, review, records and preservation of the asset, not family conflict.
  13. Avoid unsupported claims. Use language such as “suspected misuse”, “concerns regarding” and “allegations requiring investigation” unless direct proof exists.
  14. Continue building the evidence pack. After the urgent referral, collect bank records, aged-care statements, tenancy records, rebate records and care placement documents.
  15. Prepare for next-stage process. Expect requests for more documents, a hearing or urgent conference, responses from the attorney, and possible legal representation.

6. Capacity and authority pathways

A key part of the document was separating the response depending on whether the older person had legal decision-making capacity at the relevant time.

Scenario Practical pathway
If the older person has capacity Obtain independent medical and/or legal confirmation of capacity. If the person understands the property, the EPA and the proposed sale, they may revoke the EPA, appoint a new attorney, and instruct the agent or settlement parties directly.
If the older person does not have capacity Act through protective channels. Seek public advocate/tribunal review, request urgent interim protection, ask for production of records, and seek appointment of an independent administrator or suitable substitute decision-maker if required.

8. Edited sample extracts

8.1 Urgent complaint/referral extract

We write on behalf of concerned family members regarding Older Person A. The family seeks urgent review and assistance because Older Person A’s major asset is at risk of being sold before the person’s current wishes, decision-making capacity, financial circumstances and attorney conduct have been independently examined.

The family’s immediate concern is that the property may be sold and sale proceeds may become difficult to trace before appropriate oversight can occur. The family therefore requests urgent guidance about protective steps, review of the attorney’s authority, production of financial records and preservation of the asset pending assessment.

8.2 Tribunal cover letter extract

This application is brought due to serious concerns about the management of Older Person A’s financial affairs by Attorney B, and the urgent risk that Older Person A’s property may be sold before the circumstances can be properly investigated.

The family respectfully seeks urgent consideration of protective orders, including review of Attorney B’s authority, production of relevant financial records, and interim steps to prevent irreversible dealing with the property pending proper consideration by the Tribunal.

8.3 Real estate notice extract

We give formal notice that there is a serious dispute regarding the authority being relied upon to progress the proposed sale of the property. Urgent review is being sought through appropriate protective channels.

In those circumstances, the family requests that no further steps be taken to progress the sale, exchange contracts, proceed toward settlement, or destroy any records relating to instructions, authority, identity checks, agency documents, correspondence, file notes or conveyancing communications.

9. Evidence discipline and risk control

A major strength of the original document was that it did not encourage reckless accusations. It directed the family to distinguish between allegation, concern, observation and documentary proof.

  • Use “suspected misuse” unless documentary proof establishes misuse.
  • Use “concerns regarding” when the issue requires investigation.
  • Keep witness statements limited to personal knowledge.
  • Avoid broad character attacks or unsupported conclusions.
  • Prioritise dates, records, communications, documents and decisions.
  • Separate the person’s welfare from family conflict or personal grievance.

Dear Neighbour: Tell me when to call

Creative nonfiction. First published on LinkedIn. The people involved are not named.

Dear neighbour,

I’m worried.

I’m worried that lately the “F--- yoooooooooouuuuuuu” he screams at you lasts much longer than it used to.

I’m worried that, unlike your other neighbours, I know what every extra syllable means.

It is no longer a phrase. It is every supposed wrongdoing he has stored against you. Every grievance he has lodged. Every justification he is building to permit what happens next.

The longer the word, the shorter his fuse, the greater the risk.

I know too well what happens when that fuse is lit. What comes next frightens us more than we will ever confess.

Rage can be loud, but the moment it turns deadly is often quiet.

Like the night I did not recognise you.

We have been neighbours long enough for me to know your face. Yet that night, in the dark, I thought I was running towards a stranger, a cousin, a sister, an auntie or a friend.

It was not until you spoke that I realised it was you.

Now I sit on the other side of a brick wall trying to calculate the exact moment concern becomes an emergency.

Call too early and police arrive, hear calm voices, decide there is nothing to see and leave.

He always punished me after they left.

Call too late and the ambulance will have no reason to hurry.

Last time, I had to call more than once. When they finally arrived, it had taken longer than the 12-minute priority-response target. I then spent longer than that reasoning with them to make them stay.

Then I had to calmly explain to an officer who genuinely did not seem to know why you “didn’t want to press charges.”

I am scared that next time I will have to explain why you are leaving in a coroner’s van rather than an ambulance.

I have enough memories of moments when somebody should have acted sooner. I do not need another life measured afterwards through warning signs everyone suddenly claims were obvious.

I do not want you to stay here as a ghost.

I do not know whether silence respects your choices or abandons you to his.

I know the warnings about stepping in. I know the danger of interfering.

I know why.

A second injured or dead woman does not rescue the first.

I know there are risks in calling. I know there are reasons you may fear the people sent to help you. I cannot pretend those reasons are small.

Yet death does not become less final through details.

If you die, you are still dead.

You become another woman discussed in the past tense. Another statistic. Another death met with promises to improve a response that needed its hand held while it lost you.

I will intervene.

So I need a word.

Not an explanation.

Not permission.

Not a safety plan.

A word.

“Pyjamas.”

“Banana.”

“Fire.”

Anything.

Any word that tells me you have reached the point where your fear outweighs your fight.

A word that means: call now.

You should not have to carry the extra task of creating an emergency protocol for the neighbour listening through the wall.

Yet here I am asking you to do exactly that.

Because I have to.

When help arrives carrying the judgement of an officer who was taught about domestic violence but did not learn it, arriving early may permit the violence rather than stop it.

Without a word, I am left listening for the difference between another beating and your last breath.

I do not know what you want me to do.

We share no friendship, no family connection and very few words.

We share a brick wall somewhere in between two places that we hoped would be safe.

Yet I know his voice.

I know it is changing.

I know the word is getting longer.

I know your time may be getting shorter.

Dear neighbour,

Please tell me when to call.

Before the silence settles and your ghost starts paying rent.

In an emergency, call 000.

Not an emergency but worried about your neighbour? Call 1800RESPECT.

Dear Police Commissioner. Service Review: Next Time, Could I Call You?

Open letter. First published on LinkedIn.

Police Commissioner,

A copy of WA’s updated family violence legislation sits pinned to my homepage like a gold star on the State’s report card. I read the press releases. I read the policy documents. They are inspiring. Progressive.

What they are not, apparently, is instructive.

And you cannot ride on progress’s forward momentum when there is none.

There is none.

Escalating violence. My neighbour, unrecognisable. Three calls to 000. Holes in walls. Broken items. Broken bones. Named and willing witnesses.

And the words out of your officer’s mouth:

“She doesn’t want anything to do with the bloke. But she doesn’t want to press charges. I don’t get it.”

I do not need to tell you that victims do not "press charges" in this state.

That distinction matters most in family violence, don’t you agree? Particularly when fear may force a victim to blame the door for injuries she cannot hide.

Police investigate. Police decide whether to lay charges. The procedure is straightforward. Black and white. Right?

An attending officer should not need a frightened witness to explain why an injured woman may want nothing more to do with the man who assaulted her, yet still feel unable to cooperate with police.

That is not a contradiction.

That is family violence.

That night was a failure of training. Or was that night the training?

From where I stood, with the decision to leave being made, then unmade only because I refused to let it happen quietly, it did not look like an exception.

It looked like policy.

WA is changing the way it talks about family violence. You now speak about coercive control, patterns of abuse, cumulative harm and risk.

Commissioner, your officers read the scene about as well as they read the memo: fear can look like silence, denial or withdrawal.

A damaged home. A victim too scared to speak. A perpetrator no longer present. An identified and willing witness.

All of that about to be concluded with your officers, on their way out, calling into the home of the person who called them over.

Note the “about to.”

They did stay a little longer, but only after I met their ludicrous decision with an equally ludicrous question:

“Should I simply call 000 again once you go, so you come back?”

That question should not have been necessary.

I had called 000. I waited. I intervened while I waited. When the violence escalated, I intervened again, the perpetrator turned on me. I called twice more and gave precise, real-time accounts of the brutality being unleashed on my neighbour.

Yet no one asked me for a formal statement. No one left a card or a name or a number. No questions were asked about what “he turned on me” actually meant.

Between my words and her injuries, we still did not reach whatever threshold your officers needed before family violence protocols kicked in.

Perhaps it would have been had I put on headphones, gone to sleep and called in a dead body on my driveway the next morning?

The new legislation is inspiring. It is progressive.

It is not instructive.

Commissioner, six years ago, when I the victim of life changing domestic violence, I asked two of your officers:

“How can I expect my neighbour to call police when police can’t even be bothered turning up?”

I am the neighbour now.

I called.

They showed up.

And I am asking why they bothered.

Opinion · First published on LinkedIn

The Most Expensive Education in Western Australia (And Why You’re Paying For It)

First published on LinkedIn.

Last week, thousands of West Australian families were glued to the TISC website, waiting for ATAR results. We celebrated the scholars, the high achievers, and the bright futures of the Class of 2025.

Proud parents joined their children to research university entrance scores, while disappointed parents ruminated on the “what ifs.” If we had just spent $40,000 a year on private school, would he have had the option to study Medicine?

After all, we are conditioned to believe that the more money spent on a child, the better the result.

That being said, an institution costing one million dollars per year, per child, would have to be the best of the best.

And contextually speaking, it is.

State-funded and exclusive, Western Australia’s most expensive “boarding school” operates with a level of consistency that is statistically unusual.

But let’s be clear about what it produces.

These students are not being primed for law, medicine, or STEM degrees. They are being prepared to enter the demographic with the highest incarceration rate in the nation, feeding a cycle of recidivism that begins in childhood and extends into adulthood.

It is located inside the maximum-security adult Casuarina Prison.

It is called Unit 18.

It takes children sentenced in the Children’s Court, yes, sometimes more than once, and systematically turns damaged, traumatised boys into the men responsible for the crimes we later claim to be shocked by.

Waiters, Doctors, and Anaphylaxis

Why is this “school” failing so badly? Because we are using the wrong tools for the job.

Imagine you are at a restaurant and your child goes into anaphylactic shock. Their throat is closing. They are panicking. You scream for help.

Now imagine the manager sends over a waiter.

The waiter is a perfectly good employee. He knows how to carry plates and lock doors at night. But he has no medical training. He doesn’t have an EpiPen. He has a menu.

You would be outraged. You would say, “My child is dying. I need a doctor.”

The manager shrugs. “Doctors and waiters both work with people,” he says. “It’s basically the same thing.”

This is exactly how we are running Unit 18.

We have children experiencing severe trauma, cognitive disability, and suicidal ideation: psychological anaphylaxis. And who do we send in to manage them?

Prison officers.

These officers are not villains. But they are the waiters in this scenario. Their training is designed to secure adult criminals. They are taught locks, keys, and containment. They are not trained in paediatric neurology, trauma response, or child psychology.

Asking a prison guard to fix a traumatised child is like asking a waiter to perform a tracheotomy with a steak knife.

It’s unfair to the child.

It’s unfair to the officer.

And the outcome is always going to be a bloody mess.

The Tale of Two Teenagers

To understand who these “students” are, you have to look past the razor wire.

Picture your son. He’s 15, neurotypical, and now stands a foot taller than you. His exposure to violence is limited to the hour of Grand Theft Auto you allow him to play at night. His deepest psychological wound is having to do extra chores to earn pocket money for the next edition.

It’s been a long, scorching 40-degree day at school. He comes home late, hot and irritable. You ask where he’s been.

He snaps.

He tells you to “get stuffed.”

That’s teenage attitude. You might confiscate his Xbox. You might take his phone for a week.

If you were to lock him in a bathroom for 23 hours a day, cut off the water, and leave him there, you would be the lead story on the six o’clock news right after Child Services removed him from your care.

Because a 15-year-old boy is just a child with undeveloped frontal lobes responding to his situation accordingly.

Well, yes.

Your boy is.

Now picture the boy in Unit 18.

He is also 15. But he is neurodivergent, with the cognitive reasoning of a 12-year-old. His exposure to violence wasn’t on a screen; it was in his living room, his neighbourhood, his childhood.

He is also hot.

He is also scared.

And he also tells the adult responsible for him to “get stuffed.”

In Unit 18, “get stuffed” isn’t merely spoken. “Get stuffed” becomes stolen cell keys and assault charges. His behaviour is labelled inexcusable, then used to justify the inexcusable reality of the unit.

These boys make the news.

But Child Services are not coming to save them.

And these boys, they aren’t afforded the right to be assessed by the undeveloped frontal-lobe lag.

Because if they were, differentiating between “your boy” and “that boy” simply because it’s too hard to accept they are the same boy wouldn’t really fly.

The Distress Flare

We call it a “riot” because that word is useful. It conjures images of chaos, aggression, threat.

But look closer.

A boy standing on a burning roof isn’t igniting a fire.

He is drowning.

He climbs because he has spent 23 hours a day staring at a grey wall that tells him he is nothing. He knows the only time the world looks at him is when he is burning something down.

What he doesn’t know is that his distress flare is being presented as proof that he is an unmanageable, dangerous inmate.

We look at the smoke rising from Casuarina and see a threat.

We should see a child begging for the future, the dignity, and the hope we confiscated.

The only thing left to them is their scream.

And the most terrifying thing isn’t the noise they make on that roof.

It is the silence we return them to when they come down.

Right now, you might be thinking: My son’s a good boy. He’s never going to end up in Unit 18. This isn’t my problem.

And you’re right. He probably won’t.

But in five years, he might meet someone who did.

These boys do not stay in boxes forever. Eventually, the gate opens. And because we chose punishment over repair, that boy walks back into the community with no education, no rehabilitation, and a heart full of rage.

Your son might not be the criminal.

But he could be the victim of the home invasion gone wrong.

He could be the one standing across from a 20-year-old man who was brutalised by the state at 15 and now sees violence as his only language.

And when that happens, who will you blame?

Do you blame his parents for not being tough enough?

Do you blame the video games?

The drugs?

Do you blame the prison guards trained to forget the 15-year-old, six-foot-tall criminal in front of them was just a broken boy?

The tax dollars you spent condoning it?

I can’t tell you where your blame will land.

But I can tell you this:

It will be the moment you kiss your son’s casket that you finally understand.

The boy standing on that burning roof was sending out a distress flare, not trying to start a war.
