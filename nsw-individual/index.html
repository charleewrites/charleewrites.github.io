Submission · Independent advocacy

Individual submission to the NSW statutory review of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024

Prepared by: Charlee Stockden

Submitted as: Individual

Written for: NSW Department of Communities and Justice statutory review, 2026

Acknowledgement of Country

I acknowledge the Traditional Owners and Custodians of the lands on which this submission is written. I write from Whadjuk Noongar Country and acknowledge the Whadjuk Noongar people as the Traditional Owners of this land. I pay my respects to Elders past and present, and acknowledge the continuing connection of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples to Country, culture, community and family.

I acknowledge that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women and children experience family violence at much higher rates than other Australians and that this harm is bound up with colonisation, dispossession and the removal of children.

I acknowledge the victim-survivors whose experiences sit at the heart of this work, those who have shared their experiences and those who have not, those still navigating the impacts of violence, and those whose lives have been lost. I recognise the strength, knowledge and expertise that come from lived experience, including recognising children and young people as victim-survivors in their own right.

I take my direction from the people most affected by this violence. I want systems that are safer and accountable to the people they affect.

Introduction

I commend New South Wales for undertaking this review and for the well-intentioned aims of these reforms. As a victim survivor of aggravated stalking and near-fatal domestic violence, I draw on my experience where it bears directly on the questions being asked. Those who have lived through these offences see them from a vantage point that is particular to that experience. This does not mean that others cannot understand them, but it allows survivors to identify blind spots that are difficult to see from outside the experience. I therefore ask the review to give weight to that perspective.

Aggravated ADVO breach offences (Chapter 2)

Question 1: Do the policy objectives of sections 14(1A) to (1E) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain valid?

Response to Question 1:

The objective of the aggravated breach offences remains valid, but their effect depends on breaches being reported, and many are not. For every breach that is reported, many more go unreported. Protected persons are often advised to consider whether reporting is worth the risk of retaliation, because the response to a single breach is often minor compared with what may follow. I followed that advice myself, but it defeats the purpose of the order.

This also distorts the picture of domestic violence. If there are 100 protected persons and 100 recorded breaches, the figures suggest the orders are largely respected. If the actual number of breaches is 1,000 or 10,000, they show something very different.

Research on reporting supports this. The Australian Bureau of Statistics Personal Safety Survey found that 58% of women who had experienced violence from a previous partner had never contacted police about it (ABS, 2012), and ANROWS reports that 82% of women who had experienced violence from a current partner had never contacted police (ANROWS, 2018). I have not found figures for breaches of orders specifically. A study of the deaths of 151 Indigenous women and girls over 20 years also found that police were often reluctant to act on breaches of domestic violence orders (Cripps, 2023).

Question 2: Do the terms of sections 14(1A) to (1E) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain appropriate to secure their objectives?

Response to Question 2:

The offence in section 14(1C) turns on a pattern of breaches within 28 days, but a pattern can only be charged if it is reported.

I ask the review to consider how the law can respond to patterns of breach where the protected person has not been able to report every one, and whether evidence of unreported breaches can be taken into account.

Question 3: Do you have concerns about the operation of sections 14(1D) and (1E) when an aggravated offence is tried on indictment?

Response to Question 3:

I support the intention behind these provisions, which is to distinguish between breaches of different seriousness. A message saying “I miss you” is very different from a threat to kill. My concern is that when a serious breach is tried on indictment, the jury may be unable to return a verdict on the lesser breach, so a person could be acquitted outright even where the evidence shows they breached the order. I am concerned that more people may be acquitted because of the higher court procedure than convicted. That would defeat the purpose of the order, and a protected person who has reported a breach would see no consequence. The discussion paper gives no data on how often this occurs.

Question 4: If yes to Question 3, are existing procedures, including the use of back up or related charges, sufficient to address any concern?

Response to Question 4:

I am not confident that back-up charges are enough. They depend on the prosecutor laying them in every case. I also have a concern, which is not raised in the discussion paper, that back-up charges may give a defendant grounds to argue that the charging approach makes the matter harder to defend. I raise it as a question for the review and not as a finding. I ask the review to publish how many aggravated breach charges have been tried on indictment, and in how many of those the person was acquitted when the evidence showed the lesser breach.

Serious Domestic Abuse Prevention Orders (Chapter 3)

Question 5: Do the policy objectives of Part 10A of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain valid?

Response to Question 5:

The objective of the SDAPO scheme remains valid. The scheme is a constructive one, and the idea of a future-focused order that can protect people not yet known to the court has merit.

Question 6: Do the terms of Part 10A of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain appropriate?

Response to Question 6:

I am concerned that a person can qualify on the basis of a charge alone, including where they were acquitted or the conviction was quashed. I say this as a survivor of near fatal domestic violence.

Western Australia’s serial family violence offender declaration is a useful comparison, and I believe it is a fairer version of the same idea. It requires prior convictions and is made by the sentencing court at the time of a new conviction. It is discretionary, the court must consider the likelihood of reoffending, and it remains in place until cancelled, with an application possible after 10 years. Where it applies, the consequences follow a later offence: bail is refused unless there are exceptional reasons, GPS monitoring is required if bail is granted or on community orders, and firearms licences are lost. Because it rests on a record of convictions, a person whose pattern of behaviour is consistent with their character has already been identified as higher risk, and the penalties that follow reflect that. I ask the review to consider whether the NSW threshold should be tied to convictions in the same way.

Fairness is also a safety question. I do not suggest that what a person has done is acceptable. The people these orders target include some who acted violently once, out of character, and some who offend repeatedly. For a person who acted once, out of character, the label itself may make a further offence more likely. Research on domestic violence arrests suggests that when people perceive the process as unfair, subsequent violence increases, and that sanctions seen as unfair and stigmatising can provoke defiance, particularly in people with weak social bonds (Paternoster, Bachman, Brame and Sherman, 1997; Sherman, 1993). Australian research also identifies procedural justice as central to securing perpetrator compliance with police and court responses (Meyer and Williamson, 2020). In my view, a sense of injustice is more dangerous in people who already react to it with violence. A threshold that can reasonably be seen as unfair may therefore put protected persons at greater risk. I ask the review to consider evidence on how these orders affect the behaviour of the people subject to them.

Question 7: Do you have views about: the scope of conditions that may be imposed under a SDAPO, the timing requirement for service of a SDAPO application on the defendant, and/or the interaction between service requirements of a SDAPO made by the court and the SDAPO contravention offence?

Response to Question 7a:

I welcome these ideas, and the effort to tailor orders to the individual. But ideas that are not carried through in a consistent way achieve nothing. A condition only protects someone if it is monitored and enforced. The discussion paper gives examples such as limits on social media and dating apps, notification of a change of address or a new partner, and a ban on buying tracking devices. It does not say how compliance is checked.

These conditions read well on paper. However, in my experience, a person intent on monitoring or deceiving others can hold accounts under other names and can buy tracking devices without a verified identity. A condition that relies on the person to comply or to disclose will not reach those who most need to be reached.

There is evidence that tracking tools are in the hands of people subject to orders. The NSW Crime Commission has reported that one in four people who bought GPS tracking devices had a history of domestic violence (NSW Crime Commission, Project Hakea: Criminal use of tracking and other surveillance devices in NSW, 25 June 2024). In an Australian Federal Police case involving one spyware product, 14 of the 201 identified Australian buyers were respondents to domestic violence orders, and 11 of those bought it during an order or within two years of one being issued (Australian Federal Police, Imminent Monitor investigation, July 2022).

Notification conditions could be set at regular intervals, for example monthly or every three months, and varied if circumstances change, such as a new partner. I ask the review to set out how each kind of condition is monitored, who is responsible for checking compliance, and what the consequence is when a person fails to notify police of a change of address or a new relationship. I also ask the review to publish data on breaches of SDAPOs and on how they were detected, so that the effectiveness of the conditions can be assessed.

Response to Question 7b:

Fourteen days appears adequate for service of the application, provided the person can obtain an adjournment if they have not been able to get legal advice in that time. The discussion paper does not say whether this is available. Perhaps it would be more reasonable to apply a notice period similar to that for a summary offence, which I understand to be 21 days where a court attendance notice is served by post or electronically.

I ask the review to confirm that adjournment for legal advice is available in practice, and to publish how often it has been sought. I also ask the review to explain what happens where the person cannot be found to be served.

Response to Question 7c:

Where the person is present in court when the order is made, it appears reasonable for the order to take effect immediately, since they know of it. Where they are absent, personal service is the most reliable way of ensuring they know, but it can delay protection.

I ask the review to consider whether an order could instead take effect once a police officer has notified the person by telephone or text message, with the notification witnessed by a second officer and recorded in statutory declarations from both. This would give a record of when and how the person was told, which matters because the offence requires that the breach was knowing. More than one method of service is better than relying on one, because it reduces the chance that an order fails to take effect for want of a handed copy.

Amended definition of ‘stalking’ (Chapter 4)

Question 8: Do the policy objectives of section 8(1)(b1) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain valid?

Response to Question 8:

Monitoring and tracking are often treated as minor because they happen at a distance. They do not seem overtly dangerous. But for the person being watched, the distance is the source of the fear, because they cannot know when it will end. That building fear is often worse than the act itself, in the way suspense in a horror film can be more frightening than the climax. In my view stalking is much more damaging to a person’s life than most people realise.

The evidence supports this. Australian death-review data show that stalking is a significant feature of intimate partner homicide. The Australian Domestic and Family Violence Death Review Network and ANROWS found that in cases where a male primary abuser killed a female intimate partner, the male abuser had stalked the female victim in approximately 42% of cases (ADFVDRN & ANROWS, 2022). ANROWS has found that one in two Australians have experienced technology-facilitated abuse in their lifetime (Powell, Flynn and Hindes, 2022).

The objective of the amendment remains valid. Monitoring and tracking should be named as stalking. However, a law deters only when it is used and when people can see that it is used.

At present, a person who tracks another may reasonably believe that they will not be held to account. Stalking is widely treated as something that is not real or not serious, and that belief is what needs to change. When people are charged for placing a tracking device in a car or monitoring someone online, and when those outcomes are consistent and publicly known, others may think twice. It should be understood that this is not something that can be done in the dark without being found out.

The discussion paper records over 40 matters in which a stalking charge involving technology was laid between 1 December 2024 and 18 August 2026.

I ask the review to publish how many of those matters resulted in a conviction, so that it can be assessed whether the amended definition is being enforced as well as used. I also ask the review to consider how the stalking provisions can actually be carried through.

Question 9: Do the terms of section 8(1)(b1) of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain appropriate?

Response to Question 9:

The part of the definition that says stalking includes monitoring or tracking “whether or not the monitoring or tracking involves contacting or otherwise approaching the person” is excellent, because that is what this conduct is. My concern is with the words “or in another way”. Whilst wording such as this may allow police to charge in broader terms, it does not really address the issue. I ask the review to consider defining the conduct.

Queensland and South Australia already do this. Their definitions include examples such as using a tracking device or drone, checking the recorded history in a digital device, reading messages, and monitoring email, browser history or social media accounts. I ask the review to consider adding named examples to section 8(1)(b1), including conduct that does not involve technology.

Stalking is too often minimised, and I believe the incidence of domestic violence will only change when the culture around it changes. Loose wording allows the view to continue that this behaviour is not serious. A definition that names the conduct says plainly that it is wrong and against the law.

Procedural reforms for provisional AVOs (Chapter 5)

Question 10: Do the policy objectives of sections 31 and 32 of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain valid?

Response to Question 10:

The option of electronic service is valid for a protected person, and it should remain their choice. A protected person may need to produce the order quickly, for example to security staff at a shopping centre. Carrying a paper copy is a constant reminder of the abuse, whereas an order on a phone is accessible without having to look at it. I would personally have preferred to receive the order on my phone, and in my experience this is safer.

Question 11: Do the terms of sections 31 and 32 of the Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007 remain appropriate?

Response to Question 11:

I am less clear on the benefit for service on a defendant. Where an officer must already be present to explain the order and obtain consent, I do not see what electronic service adds to handing over a copy. I ask the review to examine whether electronic service on defendants is achieving its stated purpose, and to publish how often it has avoided detaining a defendant.

Any other comments

When I reported that I was being stalked, I was told that I felt it was serious only because of what had happened to me in the past, and that it was unlikely to be happening now. After serious harm occurred, police confirmed that everything I had reported was accurate.

When a report is minimised, the victim begins to doubt herself and to wonder whether she is wrong, even when she knows she is not. Serious harm often follows stalking, and when stalking is dismissed as nothing, the person is left without protection.

Laboratory research by Dr Helen Paterson has found that the more often a victim of domestic violence is victimised, the less likely she is to be believed (Paterson, reported by AAP).

I also reported that he had other people doing it for him. I was told that people do not do that. Police later confirmed that he had paid people to watch and track me. People who have done this kind of thing for payment in the past will do it again.

Had my reports been believed when I made them, serious harm may still have occurred. But I would have had some protection around me, and I would not have had to face it alone. A report of stalking should be treated as credible information about risk, and not as a reaction to a victim’s history.

I ask the review to consider how the stalking provisions can support early, serious responses to reports of tracking and monitoring, so that a victim’s account is not dismissed while the conduct is still happening.
