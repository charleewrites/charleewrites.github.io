Critique of an independent medical examination

Letter critiquing an insurer-arranged Independent Medical Examination in a workers’ compensation matter, written to support the claimant’s lawyer. De-identified: names, dates, the insurer and the hospital have been removed. Not medical or legal advice.

Letter: Critique of Independent Medical Examination

Re: [Claimant] – Independent Medical Examination Report of [Examining doctor] dated [date]

Dear [Solicitor]

I write regarding the Independent Medical Examination report of [Examining doctor] dated [date] prepared at the request of [the insurer].

Having reviewed the report carefully, there are serious concerns regarding the factual accuracy, reasoning and medical conclusions reached. In my view, the report materially understates objective evidence consistent with carbon monoxide exposure and its aftermath, and then relies on that understatement to substitute an unsupported diagnosis of Somatic Symptom Disorder.

For the reasons outlined below, the report appears vulnerable to challenge on both evidentiary and clinical grounds.

1. The report incorrectly asserts an absence of objective evidence of carbon monoxide exposure

The examining doctor states that there is:

  • “no objective evidence confirming significant carbon monoxide exposure”
  • “no documented measurement of carboxyhaemoglobin levels”
  • “no contemporaneous medical assessment at the time of the alleged exposure”.

However, the report itself records several objective matters which contradict that statement.

These include:

  • the treating hospital identifying the principal diagnosis as carbon monoxide inhalation/poisoning
  • a venous blood gas measurement recording carbon monoxide level of 6.5
  • referral for and completion of six sessions of hyperbaric oxygen therapy
  • MMSE improvement from 24/30 to 30/30 following hyperbaric treatment
  • improvement in Romberg testing following treatment

These are objective clinical findings. Regardless of how one interprets them, it is plainly inaccurate to state there was “no documented measurement” and no objective evidence.

2. The report fails to properly consider the delayed diagnosis and treatment

The chronology recorded within the report itself is significant:

  • prolonged exhaust exposure under a running vehicle (2–3 hours)
  • immediate neurological symptoms on emerging
  • presentation to hospital the same day
  • a five-hour wait without medical assessment
  • the patient leaving prior to assessment
  • later hospital review and treatment

Carbon monoxide levels decline over time, particularly once oxygen is administered. Consequently, delayed blood gas testing may underestimate the severity of the original exposure.

More importantly, delayed recognition and treatment may influence neurological outcome. The report does not adequately engage with this issue.

3. A normal MRI does not exclude carbon monoxide related neurological injury

The report places considerable reliance on the absence of MRI abnormalities.

That reliance is overstated.

While MRI can demonstrate characteristic injury patterns in some carbon monoxide exposures, the absence of such findings does not exclude neurological sequelae. Cognitive, behavioural and functional impairments may persist even where routine neuroimaging appears normal.

The absence of globus pallidus lesions or white matter abnormalities cannot reasonably be used as definitive proof that no injury occurred.

4. A normal neurological examination does not exclude functional impairment

The report relies heavily on the fact that the neurological examination on [date] was described as normal.

However, a largely normal bedside neurological examination months after toxic exposure does not exclude:

  • cognitive impairment
  • executive dysfunction
  • slowed processing speed
  • fatigue syndromes
  • neurobehavioural changes
  • subtle gait or balance impairment.

Such impairments frequently require formal neuropsychological assessment rather than routine clinical examination.

The report does not meaningfully address this distinction.

5. Objective findings recorded in the medical records are not adequately reconciled with the conclusion

The report acknowledges several findings that appear inconsistent with the final conclusion but does not adequately explain them.

These include:

  • MMSE initially 24/30
  • improvement to 30/30 following hyperbaric treatment
  • abnormal Romberg test
  • documented gait instability
  • persistent fatigue
  • observed stuttering
  • slow gait with difficulty on heel-to-toe walking
  • inability to safely complete workplace trial duties due to fatigue.

Taken together, these findings indicate that there were objective abnormalities following the incident.

The report acknowledges these findings but effectively dismisses them without proper explanation.

6. The proposed alternative diagnosis does not explain the clinical presentation

The examining doctor suggests that the claimant “probably had an episode of acute peripheral vestibulopathy”.

This hypothesis fails to explain numerous features recorded in the report, including:

  • profound drowsiness and inability to keep eyes open
  • cognitive slowing
  • memory impairment
  • speech disturbance
  • documented MMSE change
  • improvement following hyperbaric treatment
  • exertional suffocation sensation
  • new intolerance to exhaust fumes
  • persistent fatigue and functional impairment.

Peripheral vestibular disorders may explain vertigo. They do not adequately explain the broader constellation of symptoms described.

7. The diagnosis of Somatic Symptom Disorder is not properly supported

The report concludes that the claimant’s current symptoms are best explained by Somatic Symptom Disorder.

However, the report does not set out the diagnostic criteria used or demonstrate that those criteria are satisfied.

The diagnosis appears to be based primarily on the absence of MRI findings and a largely normal neurological examination. That is not sufficient to justify such a diagnosis.

Importantly, the report does not demonstrate that the claimant exhibits the type of disproportionate symptom preoccupation required to support this diagnosis.

Frustration, distress and concern about persistent symptoms following a documented workplace exposure do not, by themselves, constitute a somatic symptom disorder.

8. The report understates the recognised neurological consequences of carbon monoxide exposure

The report suggests that the persistence of symptoms across multiple domains is not typical of carbon monoxide poisoning.

This assertion is questionable.

Carbon monoxide exposure is well known to produce:

  • cognitive impairment
  • gait disturbance
  • fatigue
  • mood and behavioural change
  • executive dysfunction
  • delayed neurological sequelae.

The symptoms recorded in the report itself are not inconsistent with that spectrum.

9. The reasoning regarding work capacity is internally inconsistent

The report states that the claimant has recovered completely from any neurological injury and can return to full duties without restriction.

However, the report simultaneously states that his symptoms (attributed to Somatic Symptom Disorder) currently prevent successful return to work.

These statements are difficult to reconcile. If the claimant remains functionally impaired to the point of being unable to work successfully, that impairment requires proper clinical explanation rather than simple reclassification.

10. The occupational context has not been adequately addressed

The history recorded by the examining doctor describes:

  • prolonged exhaust exposure under a running vehicle
  • immediate onset of neurological symptoms
  • subsequent hospital diagnosis of carbon monoxide poisoning
  • hyperbaric treatment
  • objective improvement following treatment.

This sequence strongly supports the occurrence of a toxic exposure event.

The report does not adequately explain why this chain of events should be regarded as unrelated to the ongoing symptoms.

Conclusion

The difficulty with this report is not merely that it reaches an unfavourable opinion.

Rather, the report appears to:

  • minimise or mischaracterise objective evidence
  • over-rely on normal MRI findings
  • over-rely on a largely normal office neurological examination
  • fail to adequately consider delayed diagnosis and treatment
  • substitute Somatic Symptom Disorder without establishing diagnostic criteria.

In those circumstances, the report appears open to challenge.

It may be appropriate to obtain a further opinion from a specialist with experience in:

  • carbon monoxide neurotoxicity
  • acquired brain injury
  • neuropsychology
  • neuropsychiatry
  • occupational neurology.

In particular, such an expert could address whether residual cognitive and functional impairments may persist despite normal MRI findings and a largely normal bedside neurological examination.

Given the issues outlined above, the reasoning and conclusions contained in the IME report appear contestable.

Yours sincerely

Charlee

For [Claimant]
